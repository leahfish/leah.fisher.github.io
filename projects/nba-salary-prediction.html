NBA Salary Prediction: Multiple Regression Analysis

Wake Forest MSBA · Statistical Thinking for Business Decision-Making · Summer 2026

Situation

NBA front offices commit tens of millions of dollars to individual contracts, but it isn't obvious which player attributes the market is actually paying for. Working with a dataset of 566 players, my team set out to test which measurable characteristics explain salary and which are noise.

Task

Build a multiple linear regression model predicting player salary, refine it down to only statistically significant predictors, and interpret the results well enough to say something useful to a general manager.

Action

I began with a full model containing ten predictors spanning performance statistics, position, conference, age, and nationality. Working in Excel's Data Analysis Toolpak, I refined the model across six iterations, removing the single highest non-significant p-value at each step and documenting the rationale for every elimination: forward position first at p = 0.967, then US-born status, games played, conference, and finally rebounds per game. I then ran a correlation matrix on the surviving continuous predictors to test for multicollinearity, and made a deliberate choice to retain minutes per game and points per game despite a correlation of 0.87, on the grounds that each captures a distinct dimension of player value — opportunity versus scoring output — and that dropping either would discard information the other doesn't carry.

Result

The final four-predictor model explains 61.5% of the variance in player salary (adjusted R² = 0.615, F = 226.9), with every remaining predictor significant at p < 0.001. Points per game is the dominant driver: each additional point per game is associated with roughly $1.79 million in salary, and each additional assist with $1.28 million. The most interesting finding was a counterintuitive one — minutes per game carried a negative coefficient of −$423,347, which contradicts the obvious expectation that players who play more earn more. Rather than report it as a finding, I traced it to the 0.87 correlation with points per game and concluded the variable was absorbing residual variance unstably rather than reflecting a real relationship. The practical takeaway for a front office is that scoring and playmaking are the two reliable drivers of market value, while the position and minutes coefficients should not be used for contract decisions. With over a third of salary variance unexplained, factors outside the dataset — contract timing, cap constraints, injury history, draft position — likely account for much of the remainder.
